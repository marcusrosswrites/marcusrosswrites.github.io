Contacting Car Crash Victims for Your Law Firm: What the Rules Allow Before You Spend

Marcus Ross · 2026-11-23

If you own a small law firm and you're weighing a budget for accident outreach, this post is for you. It walks through two sets of official rules that shape what you can do with crash information: how Texas releases crash reports, and how the Florida Bar treats contact after an accident. Use them to see which questions to ask, then check your own state.

Where does an accident lead come from?

Every accident lead starts with a source. It might be a crash report, a public data search, or a list a vendor assembled. The source decides what you may do next.

Two questions come first. Who is allowed to see the data? And once you have a name, what does your state bar let you send, and when? A lead that answers neither question is a risk, not a bargain.

What can you get from a Texas crash report?

TxDOT is the custodian of crash records for Texas. Under Transportation Code §550.062, officers must send TxDOT a written report no later than the tenth day after a crash that involves injury, death, or property damage of $1,000 or more to any one person.

Those reports aren't open to the public online. TxDOT's crash records page says that's because of their confidential nature.

Section 550.065 lists who can get a full copy on written request and payment of the fee. That includes people involved, their authorized representatives, drivers, vehicle owners, insurers, and anyone who may sue over a death from the accident. Anyone outside the listed criteria gets a redacted copy.

A few more points from TxDOT matter for your budget:

  • A regular copy costs $6 and a certified copy costs $8. Credit card processing fees may be added.
  • TxDOT doesn't sell reports in bulk. It gives you crash ID numbers that match your request, and you buy each report one at a time.
  • It can't run searches that need confidential fields, so it can't hand over all accidents involving one person.
  • Public data can be searched with the CRIS Query Tool, and bulk crash ID requests go through an online Crash Data Request Form.
  • Records are kept for the previous 10 full calendar years plus the current calendar year.

So if a vendor tells you it has full Texas crash reports for thousands of people, ask how. The official channel doesn't work that way for the general public.

What does Florida's 30-day rule cover?

Florida shows how a state bar can limit the timing of contact. Under Rule 4-7.18(b), lawyers may not send written or electronic communications to potential clients about a personal injury, wrongful death, or other accident or disaster matter unless it happened at least 30 days before the communication. The mail checklist says the same for mailings to the affected person or a relative.

The rule reaches past paper. In January 2020, the Florida Bar Board of Governors ruled that targeted social media ads must follow the Bar's direct solicitation rules, not its general advertising rules. The Florida Bar News report adds that earlier decisions already held that online chat room posts and text messages to phones must follow those rules.

Passing the 30-day mark doesn't end your duties. Communications allowed under it are still subject to several other regulations.

What must a Florida mailing say?

Florida's direct mail checklist is a useful picture of the added cost of following the rules. A mailing prompted by a specific occurrence involving the recipient or a family member must:

  • Disclose how the lawyer obtained the information that prompted it (Rule 4-7.18(b)(2)(G)).
  • State the nature of the legal problem on the envelope, or on the outside of a self-mailer (Rule 4-7.18(b)(2)(H)).
  • Mark the envelope and each enclosure "Advertisement" (Rule 4-7.18(b)(2)(B)).
  • Include a statement of the firm's background, training, and experience (Rule 4-7.18(b)(2)(C)).
  • Disclose if a different lawyer will handle the case or the matter will be referred to another firm (Rule 4-7.18(b)(2)(F)).

If you include a contract, each page must be marked "SAMPLE" in red ink, with the signature line marked "DO NOT SIGN."

Read the Florida Bar's direct mail checklist yourself before you rely on this summary. A YES to any question means the ad doesn't comply. A NO to every question doesn't prove you comply, and the checklist doesn't replace the filing requirement in Rule 4-7.19. It was last revised August 21, 2023, so confirm it's current.

What should you ask a vendor before you spend?

Put your questions in writing and keep the answers. Good ones include:

  • Where did each name and contact detail come from?
  • Can you tell me how the information was obtained? Florida's rules can require the ad itself to say so.
  • Who sends the message, and by what channel: mail, email, text, phone, or targeted social ads?
  • Who pays for the ad? The Florida checklist treats a lawyer ad paid for by a nonlawyer as non-compliant under Rule 4-7.17(c). That matters for vendor-funded outreach.
  • Whose name appears on the ad? Rule 4-7.12(a)(1) requires the name of the lawyer, firm, or qualifying provider, including a tips or leads generator, that's responsible for it.

Mail you can't lawfully send is money wasted. Cheap leads don't stay cheap if a rule keeps you from using them.

A short checklist before you spend

Run through these before you sign anything.

  1. Data source: Can the vendor name it, and does it match what the official agency releases?
  2. State rule: Have you read your own state bar's rules on contact after an accident, including any waiting period?
  3. Contact method: Does the rule treat texts, social media targeting, and mail the same way?
  4. Required wording: Do you know what the message must say and how it must be marked?
  5. Records: Will you keep the vendor's written answers and a copy of every message sent?
  6. Responsibility: Who in your firm signs off, and who answers to the bar if there's a problem?

Rules differ by state, and these sources cover only Texas records and Florida's bar. A good habit is to ask your state bar's ethics staff or your own counsel before you commit budget to any outreach plan.
