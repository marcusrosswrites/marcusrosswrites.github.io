Car Accident in Texas? Who You Must Report It To, How Fast, and How to Get the Crash Report

Marcus Ross · 2026-11-12

If you were just in a crash in Texas, this post helps you sort out what gets reported, who can get the official report, and how fault affects your claim. It's for drivers who want the steps in plain words.

Texas sits next to several states with different rules. If you want to compare, the Find My Accident Lawyer state guide lists filing deadlines, fault systems, minimum liability limits and PIP requirements for all 50 states, so you can set Texas beside its neighbors.

Do you have to file your own crash report in Texas?

Not with TxDOT. Since Sept. 1, 2017, under Senate Bill 312, the Texas Department of Transportation no longer keeps the Driver's Crash Report, known as Form CR-2. By Jan. 1, 2019, the retention period had expired for every CR-2 form. TxDOT has none on file and can't hand you a copy.

If police didn't investigate your crash and someone gave you a CR-2 or a similar local agency form, keep it. TxDOT says to hold on to it for your own records.

Check an official source for your situation. Calling police is also smart for a practical reason. TDI says uninsured motorist coverage won't pay for a hit-and-run accident if it wasn't reported to police.

What do officers report, and how fast?

The officer carries the reporting load. Under Transportation Code §550.062, an officer who investigates a crash with injury, death, or apparent property damage of $1,000 or more to one person must send a written report to TxDOT no later than the tenth day after the crash.

TDI says the officer's report usually takes a few days to file. Get the officer's name and badge number at the scene so you can follow up.

How do you get the official crash report?

The officer's report is the Texas Peace Officer's Crash Report, or CR-3. You can buy it through TxDOT's crash report records page, which links to the Crash Report Online Purchase System. The report arrives by email after you pay.

A regular copy costs $6. A certified copy costs $8 and is the one to ask for if you need an official document, such as for a legal proceeding. Credit card transactions may carry extra processing fees.

Crash reports are confidential, and the public can't view them online. Under Transportation Code §550.065, TxDOT can release a report to anyone directly concerned in the crash or with a proper interest. That includes:

  • drivers
  • owners of vehicles or property damaged in the crash
  • insurers, including a company that covers any vehicle involved
  • people who may sue because of a death

Anyone else gets a redacted copy. TxDOT keeps crash reports for 10 years plus the current year.

When your copy arrives, read it closely. A good habit is checking the narrative, the insurance details and the description of what each driver did. If something looks wrong, contact the reporting agency.

Is Texas a no-fault state?

TDI says state law requires drivers to show proof they can pay for accidents they cause, and most people do that with liability insurance. Liability coverage pays the other driver's repairs and medical bills when you're at fault.

The same logic works in your favor. If the other driver caused the crash, their insurance should pay for your repairs.

Minimum liability coverage is $30,000 per person for injuries, up to $60,000 per accident, and $25,000 for property damage. That's called 30/60/25 coverage.

Fault can be argued. The other driver's insurer might deny its driver was at fault, claim both drivers were at fault, or say the policy limits are too low. Deadlines to sue are a separate matter, and the state comparison guide above is a place to start. For advice on your own case, talk with a licensed attorney.

How do you deal with the insurers?

Call your own insurer soon. TDI says to report an accident as soon as possible, because most companies set deadlines for filing claims. Your insurer will also call or email you to learn what happened. Under a 2021 law, companies can't renew your policy if you don't cooperate when they contact you.

If the other driver was at fault, TDI advises filing a claim with their insurer and sending it a copy of the police report. Their insurance should pay for repairs, medical bills and a rental car, but payment isn't assured. You have no contract with that insurer, so you have fewer options than you do with your own.

If the other company won't pay because it disputes fault, you can file with your own insurer. You need collision coverage, not just liability, and you'll pay a deductible. Your insurer will probably try to recover the money, and you might get your deductible back.

Once a claim is filed, TDI says the insurer must:

  • acknowledge it within 15 days of getting notice
  • accept or reject it within 15 business days of getting all needed information
  • explain any denial in writing

If it needs more time, it can take 45 days and must give a reason. The prompt payment law doesn't apply when another driver's insurer is paying, but that insurer must still act in good faith.

If a company refuses, ask for the reason in writing, with specifics. You can also file a complaint with TDI. Complaints must be in writing, and TDI can't decide who was at fault or set damage amounts. For more on how this works, read TDI's auto insurance guide and its page on dealing with the other driver's insurance.

What should you do in the first days after a crash?

Work through this list while details are fresh:

  • Photograph the scene, plus the other driver's insurance card and driver's license.
  • Note the time, date and exact location, and record weather and road conditions.
  • Collect names, addresses and phone numbers of witnesses. Independent witnesses can help prove fault.
  • If the other driver has no card, ask for their name, phone number, insurer and policy number.
  • Write down the police report number and the officer's name and badge number.
  • See a doctor or emergency room if you're hurt, and keep the medical reports and bills for your insurer.
  • Tell your insurer in writing, and keep copies.
